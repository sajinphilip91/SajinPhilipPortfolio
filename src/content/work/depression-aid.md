<div class="summary"><p><strong>The challenge</strong>Paper decision cards improved the conversation about antidepressants, but they wore out, had to be reprinted, and needed the physician and patient in the same room.</p><p><strong>What I did</strong>I led the team through physician and patient research, turned the card ritual into four design principles, and designed a shared mobile tool for the clinic and video calls.</p><p><strong>What happened</strong>In tracked testing, 97% of 23 online users and 100% of 7 offline users found the medication. Testing also exposed the problems behind the redesign.</p></div>

## 1. Understand

### Why this problem matters

Depression affects more than 264 million people worldwide and is a leading cause of disability (WHO). Choosing an antidepressant is hard: there are many options, each person reacts differently, and patients can spend months or years searching for the right medication. Patients often abandon treatment because of unrealistic expectations, lack of efficacy or side effects, while physicians tend to focus on stated effectiveness rather than the patient's wider needs.

A published study showed that simple conversation cards improved how satisfied patients and physicians were with the discussion, and how comfortable they felt with the choice.

<figure><img src="/media/work/depression-aid/167eb9f181394aad.webp" alt="A physician and patient using the paper cards. Source: KER UNIT YouTube" loading="lazy"><figcaption>A physician and patient using the paper cards. Source: KER UNIT YouTube</figcaption></figure>

### The real design question

The cards already worked, so the job was not to invent a new method. The problems were practical. Physicians had to sit beside the patient, and the cards lost their fresh look after a few presentations, so they had to be printed again.

> How might we take the cards into a digital tool without losing the human conversation that makes them work?

## 2. Define

We synthesized the research into a physician persona and a shared view of what each side expects. Both need trust, privacy and facts, but they arrive with very different emotions.

### What the paper cards do well

<figure><img src="/media/work/depression-aid/579843d8e06c48ac.webp" alt="Identified pros and cons of the physical cards" loading="lazy"><figcaption>Identified pros and cons of the physical cards</figcaption></figure>

<div class="panel"><p>What to keep, and what to fix</p><table><thead><tr><th>From the paper cards</th><th>Type</th><th>What the app does</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Talking face to face, feeling each other's emotion</td><td>Keep</td><td>The conversation stays with the physician, in person or on video</td></tr><tr><td>Easy swapping back and forth</td><td>Keep</td><td>Revisit: move between issues freely</td></tr><tr><td>Readability of the physical card</td><td>Keep</td><td>Comparison stays visual, side by side</td></tr><tr><td>Printing more cards, paper waste, cost</td><td>Fix</td><td>One digital tool, nothing to reprint</td></tr><tr><td>Updating medicines, changing the text</td><td>Fix</td><td>Content can be updated in one place</td></tr><tr><td>Marking and remembering the chosen medicine</td><td>Fix</td><td>A saved shortlist stays visible</td></tr><tr><td>Cards lost after sharing</td><td>Fix</td><td>The final medicine can be saved</td></tr></tbody></table><p>Rebuilt from the pros and cons board.</p></div>

<div class="insight"><p><strong>What we learned:</strong> The pros were human. The cons were operational, plus one cognitive problem: remembering what was chosen.</p><p><strong>What it changed:</strong> Emotional attachment became the primary challenge. Digitising the logistics was easy; keeping the human feeling was the hard part.</p></div>

We shortlisted three ideas and reviewed them with experts and 17 end users, and against technical feasibility.

<figure><img src="/media/work/depression-aid/06965bcd224a4b67.webp" alt="The three shortlisted concepts" loading="lazy"><figcaption>The three shortlisted concepts</figcaption></figure>

Screen-by-screen feedback was more useful than the pass rate. Comparing meant scrolling and forgetting earlier details, the design changed suddenly between screens, and OK and Restart led to the same screen.

### What I learned

- Listening to users' stories gave the project extra mileage. Be a good listener and observer.
- Creating a strategy is the core of a UX project, especially on a short timeline.
- Remote brainstorming should be focused and result-driven, and remote research worked very well.
- Continuous usability testing is what makes the product better.

### What this does not prove

These numbers show people could use the tool. They don't show whether patients stayed on treatment or felt better.
